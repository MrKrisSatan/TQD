# Lumen Arc Principalities

We have already seen this war end.

Elven starborne houses use predictive optics, graceful skimmers and dangerous probability magic.

Original Ashcrown faction.

## The people who survived tomorrow

The Lumen Arc Principalities descend from elven observatory fleets that studied the Crown before its fracture. Their navigators learned to perceive branching probabilities through resonant crystal. They could not see one fixed future, but could compare many incomplete ones. This gave them a reputation for impossible foresight and encouraged a disastrous belief that superior information justified superior authority.

During the fracture, the fleets saved millions by abandoning routes they knew would collapse. They also abandoned people they might have saved in less probable futures. The argument over those choices became the defining wound of modern elven politics. The Principalities now insist that prediction must be witnessed, debated and limited. Their exiled rivals, the Vesperglass Cabals, maintain that any limit on foresight is cowardice. Both remember the same catastrophe, but they preserve different lists of its victims.

## Houses, intervals and dissent

A Principality is a network of star-habitats and planetary enclaves led by a house entrusted with maintaining a particular navigational or civic function. Leadership combines hereditary continuity with formal challenge: a successor must defend decisions before citizens who may have lived long enough to remember the predecessor’s original promises. Seers advise councils but cannot legally rule by prophecy alone.

The Interval Courts examine whether a prediction was manipulated through selective disclosure. Their proceedings are slow and imperfect. Wealthy houses can afford better instruments, making unequal access to foresight a persistent political problem. Elven longevity encourages patience, but also allows grievances to survive long after their practical causes disappear. Younger citizens increasingly demand the right to choose an uncertain life instead of being protected into a destiny calculated before their birth.

## War in the space between choices

Lumen armies are small, expensive and highly trained. Prism Linekeepers deliver accurate fire, Dawnthread Blades strike where a defence is momentarily disordered, and Arcwind Cavaliers exploit rapidly changing angles. Their grav engines favour speed over heavy plating. Seers and lattice artisans are essential specialists whose loss cannot be replaced by a larger supply of ordinary recruits.

Foresight supplies a native reroll to missed firearm attacks, while Swift movement preserves their advantage in positioning. These are constrained expressions of prediction, not invulnerability. They remain vulnerable to saturation, disruption and opponents willing to accept losses the Lumen models consider irrational. Thin armour and low formation strength punish mistakes. Their greatest tactical danger is believing an elegant plan must be correct because it was expensive to calculate.

## The obligation to remain surprised

The Principalities maintain uneasy scholarly exchanges with the Sauridians and respectful arguments with the Daughters about whether a good result can justify withholding the truth. The Compact values their navigation services but distrusts unequal treaties based on secret predictions. The Vault Concord demands written contingencies for every promised future. The Synod refuses to let its forests be reduced to variables in an elven model.

War with Vesperglass is intimate and vicious because each side knows the other’s methods. The Principalities also fear Verminhorde memory theft: a captured seer could reveal not just a plan, but the discarded alternatives surrounding it. Their most controversial reform requires commanders to preserve records of failed predictions. A civilisation that deletes its mistakes from the archive can become certain of its own wisdom just as it becomes incapable of learning.

## Auralis Span

A chain of transparent habitats orbiting a dim star. Public observatories allow citizens to inspect the instruments that inform state policy.

## The Quiet Meridian

A neutral navigation station where hostile houses exchange emergency route data. Violence here is treated as an attack on every ship still travelling.

## Ilyr’s Last Garden

A planetary refuge containing plants rescued from worlds the fleets could not save. Each grove bears a plaque describing the abandoned alternatives.

## House Vaer

Reformers favour citizen line troops and open predictive archives. Their enemies accuse them of sacrificing decisive advantage to procedural conscience.

## House Quill

Fleet aristocrats specialise in grav cavalry and rapid intervention. They are often first to a crisis and impatient with councils that have not yet agreed it exists.

## The Last Interval

Seers advocate strict limits on temporal expenditure. They believe repeated manipulation can destroy futures that have not yet acquired anyone capable of defending them.

## The Unwritten League

Younger citizens reject hereditary prediction and prefer scouts, empirical intelligence and plans that admit uncertainty. Some accuse them of throwing away their people’s greatest gift.

## Strategarch Aelith Vaer

Aelith published the probability tables behind a celebrated victory and revealed that a neighbouring colony had been quietly written off. Her house nearly expelled her before the colony’s survivors made her their protector. She commands through shared situational awareness, expecting subordinates to challenge predictions. Her political project is a Principality whose citizens can knowingly refuse the future their rulers recommend.

## Ilyr Sen of the Last Interval

Ilyr remembers several histories that never became real. This makes him wise about consequences and unreliable about ordinary chronology. He once prevented a civil war by destroying the instrument that proved he would win it. He now hunts evidence that too much predictive intervention is narrowing the entire species’ future into a small set of increasingly violent possibilities.

## Admiral Thessa Quill

Thessa built her reputation by taking a relief fleet through a route every model rated unacceptable. She saved the target habitat and lost the flagship carrying her family. Neither fact cancels the other in her mind. Her cavalry doctrine creates several simultaneous dilemmas, forcing opponents to make choices too quickly for even a seer to calculate comfortably.

## 3 AC — The Choosing of Routes

Observatory fleets escaped the fracture through selected corridors. Their rescue decisions created the moral and political division that later split the elven people.

## 96 AC — The Trial of Unseen Lives

The first Interval Court convicted a prince for hiding an unfavourable prediction from an ally. The ruling established that prophecy did not erase responsibility.

## 310 AC — The Battle of Three Tomorrows

Lumen and Vesperglass fleets each predicted the other’s victory. Thessa forced an unmodelled evacuation, saving civilians while both military plans collapsed.
