# Riftbound Ascendancy

Become the weapon your masters feared.

Northern covenant fleets and manifested demons fuse abyssal plate, unstable flesh and pact-driven siege engines.

Original Ashcrown faction.

## The north beyond the map

The Riftbound Ascendancy arose in northern systems whose Crown anchors failed outward, opening persistent passages into a dimension of mutable identity. Their people survived by negotiating with entities that could alter flesh, gravity and memory. The first pacts were practical: warmth through a lethal winter, protection from radiation, a navigable path between isolated worlds. Later rulers discovered that the same bargains could purchase conquest.

The Ascendancy is not a single demon cult. It is a hierarchy of mortal jarldoms, covenant hosts and manifested powers bound together by contracts no party entirely trusts. Its ideology teaches that fixed limits are tools of domination and that transformation is the highest form of sovereignty. In practice, the stronger signatory usually writes the terms. A warrior escaping one master may spend the rest of life becoming a weapon for another.

## The law of the pact

Northern communities preserve long oral histories and complex hospitality traditions. Their mortal populations are not uniformly malicious, and many live under covenants inherited from ancestors who faced extinction. The Ascendancy’s ruling warlords exploit these obligations, claiming that successful expansion will eventually buy freedom from the demons. The demons promise the same thing in carefully incompatible language.

Names, scars and witnessed deeds anchor a pact. Chainwrights maintain the machinery that gives a demon physical stability; witches interpret the conditions under which it may act. Mutations can be chosen enhancements, unintended consequences or punishments disguised as gifts. The faction’s deepest social division separates those who want better contracts from those who want to abolish the entire relationship. Neither can easily survive long enough to prove the other wrong.

## An army that changes during the war

Oathscar Reavers and Abyssforged Knights form the disciplined mortal core. Hellwake Riders break lines, while unstable thralls and manifested demons attack where ordinary biology would fail. Pact Furnaces and Breachmouth guns combine real industrial machinery with entities that alter the behaviour of ammunition. Covenants provide cohesion, but every command must account for the motives of the beings carrying it out.

Mutable Flesh increases a formation’s weapon damage as it destroys enemies, rewarding momentum without granting unlimited growth. A commander must feed the right units opportunities while preventing the army from chasing kills instead of securing the field. The faction has strong assault power but inconsistent accuracy and expensive heavy assets. Its political weakness is transactional loyalty: a bad campaign invites subordinates to renegotiate the terms under which they remain subordinate.

## The conquest of possibility

The Ascendancy views the Crown as a prison built to keep reality stable. It seeks enough relays to make transformation permanent and remove the distinction between ordinary space and the realm beyond. This brings it into conflict with nearly every power that depends on predictable physical laws, including the Vesperglass Cabals, whose profitable uncertainty still requires customers to remain recognisable.

Its relationship with the Hollow Star is rivalry rather than simple alliance. The Ascendancy celebrates becoming; the Convent seeks ownership through vows. Demonic beings can serve either, but a patron that appears in both systems will attempt to make the contracts collide. The Daughters oppose both while recognising that a frightened northern civilian is not the same as the warlord who sold that civilian’s future. Hornbound recruiters and Dawn rescue missions threaten the Ascendancy by offering escape without a new supernatural master.

## Veydr’s Reach

A cold system of oath-halls, industrial fjords and unstable auroral rifts. Its inhabitants measure seasons by both temperature and the reliability of natural law.

## The Ninth Wound

A permanent breach surrounded by covenant fortresses. No map of its interior remains accurate for more than one negotiated passage.

## Chainfall

A forge moon where living smiths and bound entities construct pact engines. The oldest factory contract is longer than the city’s legal code.

## The Storm Jarldoms

Mortal northern rulers favour disciplined reavers and heavy knights. They claim to command demons as allies while carefully avoiding the question of who can terminate the agreement.

## The Ninth Covenant

Witches and jurists believe survival depends on exact interpretation. Their archives contain examples of disasters caused by a single ambiguous pronoun.

## The Unfinished Host

Manifested demons and enthusiastic transformists reject stable identity. They are powerful assault troops and notoriously poor custodians of conquered infrastructure.

## The Severed Chain

Rebel covenanters seek freedom from inherited pacts. Some fight beside outsiders; others believe only a larger, more destructive bargain can break the old one.

## High Jarl Korr Veydr

Korr inherited a kingdom whose winters were kept survivable by a demon demanding annual military service. He expanded that service into a campaign of conquest, insisting each captured relay brings independence closer. His people admire his courage and have begun to question why the promised release is always one war away. He is an effective combined-arms commander because he understands the limits his speeches deny.

## Myrka of the Ninth Wound

Myrka survived nine supposedly fatal injuries by renegotiating her personal covenant. Each recovery removed something from her previous identity. She records her decisions in an external journal so that the person she becomes can be held accountable by the person she was. Her magic is formidable, but her real ambition is to discover a form of healing that does not require a creditor.

## Tharoz the Unfinished

Tharoz manifests differently to every witness but consistently remembers the promises made to it. It regards permanent form as a mortal superstition and takes delight in forcing adversaries to revise their understanding mid-battle. Unlike many patrons it openly admits that its concept of liberation may destroy the people receiving it. Korr respects this honesty more than he should trust the being behind it.

## 11 AC — The Winter Bargains

Northern colonies made emergency pacts after their reactors and orbital supplies failed. Later governments converted temporary survival contracts into inherited obligations.

## 173 AC — The War of Broken Names

A jarl attempted to nullify every covenant by renaming his people. The resulting conflict established that identity was more than a label and considerably harder to escape.

## 305 AC — The Siege of Chainfall

Rebels nearly destroyed the forge moon’s master contract. Korr saved the factories, then discovered that preserving them had renewed obligations he had intended to end.
